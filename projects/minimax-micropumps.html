Micropumps small enough to swallow

Two years as a research assistant in UT's MINIMAX Lab. Most of my hours went into storyboarding the lab's interactive textbook chapters, screen by screen, for the developers who build them; the most interesting slice was an experimental piezoelectric micropump that I was solely responsible for.

ConstraintsIngestible scale; efficiency mattered because everything runs on what fits inside the capsule

The pump work

A pump you swallow has almost no room for a battery, so the energy each stroke wastes decides whether the concept works at all. The lab's approach uses a piezoelectric element to drive fluid through microfluidic channels, and the experimental micropump was mine alone: I modeled the channel geometry in SolidWorks, changed it, and measured what the change did on test fixtures I designed for the purpose. By the end my prototypes were moving fluid at 1.6x the energy efficiency of the commercial pump I benchmarked against.

The honest ending: the pump stayed experimental. It did not make it into the current generation of the lab's pill robot, and I have since left the lab. I still count it as the most interesting thing I did there, because every iteration had a number attached to it.

The textbook work

Time-wise, this was most of the job. The lab publishes interactive textbook chapters built in Unity by an outside company, and those developers need to know exactly what every screen does before they can build it. I storyboarded four chapters end to end, about 120 slides: hydraulic pumps, unconventional actuators (shape memory alloys, dielectric elastomers, electrostatic and magnetostrictive actuators), displacement sensors, and mechanism design.

Every slide mocks up the module screen, and I set up a color code so the developers could read each one without asking me anything: black for text that appears on screen, green for what the user clicks or drags, red for what moves or animates in response, blue for notes to the build team. The developers were spread between Clemson, Turkey, and China; we synced in a handful of cross-site meetings over the year, but with that distance the documents mostly had to stand on their own, since a question that waits overnight for an answer stalls a chapter. Writing a spec another team can build from without follow-up questions turned out to be its own engineering skill.
