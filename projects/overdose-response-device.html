A device that recognizes an overdose and tells you what to do

A working prototype that watches pulse, blood oxygen, and breathing sounds, decides when they look like an opioid overdose, and then talks an untrained bystander through naloxone (Narcan) and calling for help, one instruction at a time.

OutcomeWorking bench prototype: three sensor inputs aggregated into a risk score that selects what the bystander is told to do. Not a medical device, not clinically validated
My roleDesigner and builder: sensor selection, detection logic, firmware, enclosure, prompt flow
ConstraintsMust not trip on one noisy signal; prompts must be followable by someone who has never seen Narcan
SkillsESP8266 firmware, MAX30100 pulse oximetry, audio sampling, risk scoring, LCD and alert hardware

Why it exists

Opioid overdoses are survivable if someone nearby recognizes what is happening and acts within minutes: give naloxone, call emergency services, support breathing. The failure is usually recognition and hesitation, not availability of the drug. I wanted a device that removes both, by detecting the physiological signature automatically and then giving the person in the room one instruction at a time.

Respiratory depression is the defining sign, so a microphone records the breathing pattern, and a MAX30100 supplies pulse rate and SpO2. An ESP8266 combines the three into an aggregate risk score, and that score, not any single reading, decides which instructions the secondary user (the person administering Narcan) is given.

The hard part was not reading the sensors, it was deciding when to believe them. A pulse oximeter on a moving person produces plenty of false drops, and a microphone in a room hears a lot that is not breathing. Scoring the three together, rather than acting on any one of them, is what made the prototype usable, and it is the same lesson I later applied to counting cycles on a test rig: only count what you have confirmed.

Technical details

SensingMAX30100 pulse oximeter for pulse rate and SpO2; microphone recording the breathing pattern
ControllerESP8266; firmware aggregates the three inputs into a risk score that selects the instruction set
OutputTwo 16x2 character LCDs: live readings on one, step-by-step Narcan prompts on the other; mini buzzer and LEDs for the alert; simulated 911 call over Wi-Fi (dummy endpoint, intentionally not live)
Enclosure3D printed
StatusWorking first prototype, since partly disassembled for parts. Not a medical device; not clinically validated
